Your Game Is Finished. Stop Touching It.

28 November 2026

Every project has a moment where it is done. Nobody notices. There's no bell, no certificate, no small parade. The game simply reaches a state where a player can start it, do a thing, and reach an end, and the maker is already thinking about the next feature.

That's the moment the project starts dying. I've watched the pattern enough times to state it plainly: small games don't fail at the beginning. They fail in the middle of being nearly finished.

The One More Feature Tax

Adding a feature is cheap to say and expensive to survive. A new puzzle needs a room, the room needs a reason to exist, the reason needs a hook, the hook needs an item, the item needs a place to hide, and the hiding place needs a hint, and the hint needs testing. One feature is a small city.

Worse, each addition rearranges what already worked. The quick fix in the corner becomes a load-bearing wall. You are not adding to a game anymore. You are renovating a house while standing inside it.

The tax is invisible because it's paid in enthusiasm. The first feature is free. The fourth is a bill.

Polishing Is a Place You Can Live

Polishing feels exactly like progress, which is its crime. The title screen gets a shine. The font gets a second opinion. The sound that plays when you open a door gets three hours of attention and one earworm later, still plays when you open a door.

None of this is wrong. All of it is fun, which is the problem. A finished game with a rough title screen is a game. An unfinished game with a beautiful title screen is a title screen with ambitions.

Here is the test I use: can a stranger reach the end without me in the room? If no, polish is a lovely way to avoid the actual work. If yes, start the clock, because you are now in the final stretch whether it feels like it or not.

Boxes, Not Vibes

Time-boxing gets a bad reputation because it sounds like a schedule, and schedules sound like homework. It isn't a schedule. It's a fence.

Pick the thing that must exist for the project to count as finished. Not the thing that would be nice. The thing that, if absent, leaves a hole where the game should be. Write it down. Give it a week, not a date. When the week ends, whatever stands there is the game.

The fence isn't cruel. It's the only way to see the edge of the field. Without it, every feature is equally urgent and nothing is ever done, which is the exact condition of every project that stalls for years and then quietly disappears.

Who Pays for a Small Thing

Small projects get funded in small ways: a modest budget, a modest price, a modest hope. The math is blunt. If a project takes a year of evenings, it cannot be priced like a weekend. If it's priced like a weekend, it can only take a weekend.

So either the scope shrinks or the price rises or the project takes a year of evenings and the maker keeps the receipts in a drawer marked character building. All three are honest choices. Pretending otherwise is how a hobby becomes a grudge.

The real funding question isn't money. It's attention. A tiny project that finishes and gets played beats a grand one that doesn't. A finished thing can be bought. An unfinished thing can only be described.

Why Projects Stall

A project stalls the day it starts being about two games: the one being made and the one being imagined. The imagined one is always better, because it has no bugs.

Then a feature gets added to close the gap. Then the feature adds a bug. Then the bug gets fixed with a second feature. Somewhere in there the maker looks up and finds they're building the imagined game through sheer attrition, one evening at a time, and the original idea is a footnote.

Finishing Is a Decision

Finishing is not a reward for good behavior. It's a call you make before the evidence is in. At some point you close the file, name what's there, and accept that the title screen has a typo and the door still creaks, and the typo is now part of the game.

That's the whole trick. Not motivation. Not discipline. Just noticing that you passed the finish line three features ago and kept building anyway.
