The Audience Didn't Shrink. It Got Absorbed.

28 November 2026

Every so often someone asks why the adventure game stayed a niche while its ideas went everywhere. The question carries a sigh. A form that taught games to talk, to hold a plot, to make an object matter, and it ended up with a small, devoted, slightly defensive audience. What happened.

Two answers get offered. They are not quite opposites, but they pull in different directions.

The patience argument

The first says the audience was trained out of it. Adventure games ask you to stop, read, think, try something, fail, and think again. That is a rhythm closer to working a crossword than to the rest of the medium. The games that grew up around the genre taught people that a screen should reward reflex, that a pause is a mistake, that the correct response to being stuck is to move faster. A form built on dwelling lost its people to forms built on flow. Nobody decided this. It was just where the training went.

This is a fair argument, and it is a little self-flattering. It assumes a patient golden age that may never have existed. Plenty of people bounced off the old games not because they were too fast for them but because the games were unforgiving and cryptic and occasionally cruel. Friction is real. It is not the same as depth, and the genre has always had trouble telling the two apart.

The absorption argument

The second answer is the more interesting one. The audience never shrank. The techniques simply left home.

Look at what an adventure game actually invented: the idea that a place can hold a puzzle, that an object can be a sentence waiting to be finished, that dialogue can be a mechanism and not a cutscene. Now look at everything else. Survival-minded games make you combine what you find. Stealth games turn a room into a set of interlocking conditions. Even shooters route you through locked doors toward a thing that opens other things. These are adventure-game thoughts wearing other clothes. The people who might once have played one kind of game now play six, and the six quietly absorbed the lesson.

So the audience didn't leave. It dispersed, and it took the vocabulary with it. Which makes the genre's smallness strange. A form that lost its players to a hundred successors should feel vindicated, not diminished. Instead it feels like a parent whose children are doing very well and never call.

What the two views miss

Both accounts treat the size of the audience as the interesting fact. It may not be. The interesting fact is that adventure-game thinking became the substrate of almost everything else while remaining, in its own right, a taste rather than a default.

That is not a failure. Plenty of forms are substrates. Poetry is a substrate for every song lyric and almost nobody reads it by choice. The adventure game has the same deal: everyone uses its tricks, relatively few want the concentrated version, and the concentrated version is the whole point. You do not get the pleasure of a lock that is also a question from a game that merely borrowed the lock.

The case against

Before this gets too comfortable, consider the opposing view. Perhaps the absorption story is a comforting myth. Perhaps the audience really did leave, and what got absorbed was only the shallow parts: keys, doors, inventories, the visible machinery. The slow, argumentative heart of the thing, the part that makes you sit with a problem and negotiate with a stubborn world, did not spread anywhere. It stayed behind, in a small room, with a small number of people, and calling that dispersal is a way of not saying decline.

It is a strong point. Inventories travel easily. Patience does not.

The thing worth noticing

Here is the uncomfortable middle. If the audience is small because the form asks for something the rest of the medium has stopped asking for, then the size is not a problem to be solved. It is the form doing its job. A thing that demands patience will always have fewer takers than a thing that demands nothing. That is not a marketing failure. It is a description.

The genre could chase the crowd, soften the friction, borrow back the reflexes it never had. Some of it has. The result is pleasant and forgettable, which is worse than small. Or it could accept that its audience didn't vanish but scattered into a hundred games that no longer say the word adventure, and that a few of those people will come back, eventually, wanting the concentrated version.

The storytelling got everywhere. The sitting-still did not. That is the whole story, and it is less sad than it sounds.
