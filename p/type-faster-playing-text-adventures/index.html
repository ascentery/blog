Want to learn how to type fast on the keyboard? Forget typing apps, just play a command-input adventure games or MUD.

28 November 2026

Typing tutors sell speed as the goal. Rows of letters, a timer, a percentage. You sit down to practice and your hands learn that typing is a chore with a score attached.

Then you close the tab and go back to two-fingered poking at a search bar.

The problem is not that the drills are wrong. It is that they have nothing on the other side. Nothing happens when the word appears on screen. A command-input adventure fixes that quietly: your fingers have somewhere to go, and the speed shows up as a side effect.

The loop that actually builds speed

A text adventure or a MUD moves at the pace of your commands. You look around, pick something up, try a direction, read a description, type again. The whole game waits on your hands.

That produces a kind of practice no app can schedule for you:

  • Repetition with a reason. You will type north and inventory and take lamp dozens of times in an evening, not because a screen told you to, but because the story keeps needing them. Muscle memory builds on meaning.
  • Whole words, not letter soup. Drills feed you nonsense strings. A game needs real vocabulary, real sentence shapes, real word boundaries. You learn to type the way you actually write.
  • The cost of a typo is visible. Misspell a command and the world does nothing, or does something strange. The correction is instant and it sticks far better than a red squiggle in a practice window.
  • No stopping point. A lesson ends after five minutes. A game ends when you decide to stop, which is usually later than you planned.

Where the speed genuinely comes from

Here is the part typing apps miss. Speed is not finger strength. It is the disappearance of the pause between thinking of a word and sending it. When the word is on its way to somewhere interesting, the pause shrinks by itself. Nobody has to tell you to relax your hands.

After enough evenings of navigating rooms and negotiating with puzzles, common verbs become reflex. You stop hunting for the keys and start watching the story instead. The letters go through your fingers without being individually approved first. That is the whole trick, and it never needs a WPM counter to happen.

MUDs add something extra: other players, live, typing at you. Answering a real person in a shared world trains the specific speed that matters, which is the speed of having something to say and getting it out before the moment passes.

Some things to keep in mind

  • Do not fix your eyes on the keyboard. The screen is where the interesting part is. Let your hands be wrong for a while; they will catch up.
  • Type whole commands, not abbreviations, at first. Shortcuts save keystrokes but skip the practice.
  • Play something you would want to read. If the world does not pull you forward, the typing stops being incidental and becomes the chore again.
