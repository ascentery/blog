The Fae Grounds Paints a Garden You Never Noticed

27 November 2026

The obvious question, the one nobody seems to ask about a garden: where does it actually end? Most gardens have an edge you can find. A hedge, a fence, a place where the mown grass gives up and the wild stuff begins. The Fae Grounds starts from the other side of that question. You are out on a usual stroll when a path appears that was not there before, and the whole game is the answer to a thing you had stopped wondering about years ago.

A garden that keeps going

The Garden Gate is the hinge. Iron gate, mossy cobblestones, overgrown hedges, the smell of damp earth and nightshade, and the same walk taken most afternoons. East of it, the ordinary stops applying. Oakfall is deep in golden leaves, thick with acorn caps and mossy stones, every step audible. The Old Oak fills its clearing with a trunk a dozen people could not span. Bramble Edge arches blackberries and thorns over a path that leads into huge mossy trunks and mist. This is a garden extended past its lease, still recognisably a garden, still the kind of place where someone hangs washing.

That last part matters. Boughlevard is a walkway strung with lines of tiny shirts, stockings and leaf-aprons, baskets travelling up and down on ropes. Crown Market sits on a bough as wide as a village green, stalls of bark and woven twigs under awnings of leaf and patched cloth. Beech Roots crowds a whole village of gnome cottages into the roots of a beech, whitewashed stone and moss-thatch. The Fae Grounds is not a garden with fairies in it. It is the domestic life of a garden, scaled down and carried on regardless of the visitor.

What the pictures are doing

The game draws its world as paintings, not pixel art. Rooms read as illustration: dappled gold through the branches, silver-green grass, the white and cream petals of the Court's giant flowers standing like furniture around a throne of living roots. The palette leans on moss and bark and leaf, and against all that green it spends colour carefully. The glowing orb and the silver locket are the two brightest things in the game, which is fitting, since they are the two things missing. Red shows up where it earns its place: rowan berries in clusters, small toadstools peeping from roots, a wide red cap on Granny Morel, the fat red kettle on the hearth in the gnome cottage.

Mood comes from damp and age rather than sparkle. Fern Hollow is a green sea of fronds beaded with water. The hollow trunk of a fallen tree is worn smooth as driftwood. Queen Maeve sits pale and still in a mauve cloak, twisting a strand of moonlight between her fingers, and the picture has to hold that stillness, not movement.

Folk who are mostly quiet

The fae here are small and shy, and the writing respects it. Nettle the pixie crouches by Mrs. Fennel's door, wringing his cloak and saying sorry in pieces. The pond sprite gives a word or two and swallows a giggle. A foxglove fairy is a trembling bell and a shifted leaf. Against them sit the loud ones: Old Tuck grumbling and meaning none of it, a hobgoblin hunched in the brambles with nobody asked me, Barnaby the brownie bright with the pleasure of a good bargain.

Folklore usually makes this court grand and dangerous. Here the quarrel between Queen Maeve and Elder Elm, the missing orb and locket, the lost young ent Rowanling, feel like a village argument with a hole in it. The task is plain: find what was lost, bring home the one who is lost.

Where that leaves the missing things is for you to find. What can be said is that the game trusts its pictures to carry the loss. An unlit court and an empty place at a door say more than any speech would.
