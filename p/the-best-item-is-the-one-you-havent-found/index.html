The Best Item in the Game Is the One You Haven't Found

28 November 2026

Every adventure game I have ever loved has a moment I dread more than any monster: the moment my bag is full. I don't mean the moment it's mechanically full, with the little red "you cannot carry any more" message, which I treat the way a chef treats a smoke alarm, that is, by ignoring it until it changes pitch. I mean the moment when I look at my inventory and it is, in some sense, done.

Here is the claim, and you can see it from the cheap seats: the most valuable object in an adventure game is the absence of one. The empty slot is the engine. The rest is luggage.

Let me break it down like a mechanic laying out parts on a clean bench.

Part one: an object is a promise with a handle

When a game hands me a brass key, it is not handing me metal. It is handing me a question in advance: which door, and when. The key is a coupon for a future I haven't been to yet. That's why finding an item is such a good feeling, and why it feels objectively useful even when the game has not yet told me what it's for. My inventory is a stack of unanswered questions, and unanswered questions are the best thing a story can be made of.

Now watch what happens when I use the key. Click, the door opens, and the brass thing vanishes from my list. In most games this is a loss, and the loss is disguised as a reward. I solved the puzzle, yes. But I also spent the coin. The inventory shrinks by one, and the world shrinks with it. Every solved puzzle is a small closing of possibility. The brilliant ones hide this; the boring ones do it in plain sight and then hand you a gemstone shaped exactly like the next door.

Part two: the empty bag is where the game gets to talk about your hopes

Here's the part I find genuinely interesting. When my inventory is empty, the world is at maximum size. Any object I find could still be anything. The description of a corridor with a locked cabinet in it is a different sentence to a player carrying no key than to a player carrying the key. The game hasn't written two halves of the puzzle; it has written one half and let me supply the other by being the kind of person who is still looking.

This is why a full inventory feels weirdly melancholic near the end of a game, and I don't think I'm being precious about a folder of adjectives. Think of the old-fashioned detective where you've collected the torn letter, the train ticket, the discredited watch, and you're meant to be triumphant. You are not. You are holding a solved argument. The fun was being a person with a pocket full of maybes.

Designers figured this out long before I did. Some games deliberately load you with junk you know is junk, because the knowing is part of the joke. Some let you keep the first object you ever found, the one with no function at all, a memento, a brick, a bit of string, and it stays in the list forever like a childhood souvenir in a drawer. That object is not doing a job. It is keeping the empty space company.

Part three: so the good designer plays the absence

If my claim is right, then the most interesting design question in the form is not "what does the player need to find?" It's "what should the player not have yet, and for how long?"

The player who is missing the item is an explorer. The player who has the item is a courier. The first player is reading every sentence in the room; the second is walking quickly. That's two entirely different games happening on the same screen, and the game switches between them the instant I put something in my pocket. I'm not saying games should starve you. I'm saying the starvation is where the story lives, and the feast is where it tidies up.

Which brings us to the callback. Remember the moment your bag was full, the moment I said I dread? I've changed my mind. The full bag is fine; it just means the ride is slowing down. The moment I actually dread is the next one after that: the game offering me a new empty slot. Because the empty slot means the game is saying, with a straight face, "I have more for you." And I will take the bait every single time, knowing full well it's bait, the way I take a second biscuit. The biscuit is not the point. The reaching is.

The next time you open that list and worry it looks sparse, don't. Sparse is the setup. Every item you haven't got yet is the hint, and the hint is the only part of an adventure game that never gets spent. Some of this is written by a machine, some by a person, but the budget of missing things is what makes the world feel big, and no tool can hand you that in a bag.
