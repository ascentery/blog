Every Room Needs One Thing You Can't Use

28 November 2026

Every adventure game hands you the same silent promise: pick it up, it will matter. A bent spoon. A rubber band. A postcard from a place the game will never let you visit. You take it because the game said take it, and then you carry it for six hours like a stone in your shoe.

Here is my claim: that useless object is the best-designed thing in the game. Not the key, not the crowbar, not the one item the puzzle clearly wants. The junk. The junk is load-bearing.

The key is a sentence. The junk is a language.

A key works like punctuation. You find it, you use it, the door stops being a problem. It is efficient and it is boring, in the way that a full stop is boring. The player never doubts the key. The key was always going to be a key.

A dead battery is a paragraph. It says: this world contains things that had a life before you arrived, and some of them are finished. It says the designers were willing to spend your inventory space on atmosphere rather than progress. It says, quietly, that not everything is a clue, which is the only reason finding an actual clue feels like anything at all.

Take that away and you get a world where every object is useful, which means every object is a lock, which means the room is a checklist. Nobody has ever felt immersed in a checklist.

The counterargument, which is fair

Players will tell you, loudly and correctly, that useless items are a betrayal. Point-and-clicks are notorious for it. You combine everything with everything because once, in some game, a rubber chicken opened a door, and now you trust nothing. The junk trained a generation to brute-force their inventories. That is a real cost, honestly incurred.

The opposing view is strongest here: a game that puts decoration in the inventory is lying about what an inventory is for. In a survival-minded roguelike, every slot is precious and a useless item is an insult with a weight value. Those players are not wrong. They are playing a different game, one where the bag is a budget.

But an adventure game's bag was never a budget. It is a pocket. Pockets hold lint. That is what makes them pockets.

What the roguelike knows

Roguelikes solved this with a beautiful cruelty: they made the junk do something, just not the thing you wanted. A cursed ring. A potion you drank too early. The item is not useless, it is a decision you have already made and cannot take back. That is a different pleasure — the pleasure of consequence — and it is a fine one.

MUDs went another way. They filled the world with objects nobody will ever need and let you carry all of it, because the point was never the puzzle. The point was that the world was big enough to hold clutter. Type the command, get a description of a chipped mug, feel the room get a little more real. Immersion by abundance.

Three communities, three answers to the same question: should the world contain things that do not serve you? Roguelike says no, but it will hurt you. MUD says yes, endlessly. Adventure game says yes, and then makes you carry it.

The point hiding in the joke

Here is the real argument. A game is immersive when it convinces you that it exists whether or not you are winning. Junk is the cheapest possible proof of that. It costs the designer almost nothing and it tells the player: this place was not built around your progress. It was built around itself, and you are visiting.

That is why the empty slot makes the found object sing, and why the useless object makes the useful one mean something. A world where everything is a key is a world with one door.

So carry the bent spoon. It will never open anything. It is opening the room.
