Nobody Reads the Room Like a Badly Lit Room

28 November 2026

I have a theory about adventure games, and it is this: the best ones are badly lit. Not visually. Structurally. The room is dark, half the furniture won't show up until you find the lamp, and you spend the first ten minutes tripping over a rug you cannot see. That darkness is not a flaw. It is the entire engine.

I don't mean literal darkness, though literal darkness does lovely work. I mean the design move of withholding the room. You enter a space and it tells you about a quarter of what is in it. The rest is a promise. The other three quarters are standing just offstage, clearing their throats.

The Unseen Hallway

Here is the thing your brain does with an incomplete room: it furnishes it. You are told there is a desk and a door. Your mind, being helpful to a fault, installs a drawer in the desk before the game has said a word about drawers. So when the game eventually mentions the drawer, you feel clever rather than informed. You guessed. You were right. Well done, you. You did the game's job for it and got the credit.

That is not a bug. That is a con on the most flattering possible terms. The game hid the answer and let you believe you invented it.

Half a Sentence

Text adventures do this with grammar. A room description is a sentence with its most interesting noun removed. The parser waits. You type a verb into the gap, and the gap fills in, and the world moves a half-step. Point-and-click games do the same trick with a cursor: an empty hotspot is a blank your eye keeps going back to, like the one light left off in a hallway.

Adventure games are the only games I know that are improved by not showing you their hand. A shooter that hides the enemy is badly programmed. An adventure that hides the candlestick is doing its job.

The Cost of Turning the Light On

There is a cost, of course. Every room you fully describe, you have said goodbye to. The moment a room tells you the drawer is stuck, the drawer stops being a possibility and starts being a chore. The mystery becomes a task. The game trades wonder for a to-do list.

This is why i find the middle of so many adventures so much wider than the beginning and the end. The beginning has the whole room to suggest. The end has the whole room resolved. The middle is where a badly lit room has to be relit one object at a time, and each lamp you find is both a gift and a loss.

The best designers know this. They keep a room or two dark on purpose. Not to annoy you. To leave your imagination something to sit in.

So Darkness Is Good, Then

I'll grant the objection: darkness without a plan is just an unlit room with a rug in it. Withholding works only when the withheld thing is worth finding. A missing noun is a joke waiting to land. A missing noun that never lands is a petty theft.

The good badly lit room always has the drawer in it. It just doesn't say so yet. And when the drawer finally opens, it holds exactly the thing you were starting to suspect it held, which is the most satisfying kind of all.

So if you want to understand why these games feel generous when they are, technically, keeping secrets from you: it's because a poorly lit room invites you to live in it, and a fully lit room only asks you to tidy it. The dark is the invitation. The rug is your problem.
