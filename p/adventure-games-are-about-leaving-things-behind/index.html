Adventure Games Are About Leaving Things Behind

27 November 2026

Every adventure game eventually asks you to carry something. A key, a rope, a note, a stone with a face on it. The convention is so old it has stopped looking like a choice: you pick it up because the game let you, and a game that lets you do something is usually telling you to do it. Pick up the thing. The thing will matter.

This is mostly true. It is also the reason so many of these games feel heavier than they are.

The inventory is an argument

Consider what an inventory actually is. It is not a pocket. It is a list of open obligations. Every object in it is a sentence the game has begun and not finished: a door you have not found for the key, a person you have not met for the note. The list grows faster than you can close it, and the closing is the game.

So the inventory is never neutral. It is a running record of what you owe the world, and the world does not tell you which debts are real. The rope might open the well. The rope might be scenery that happened to fit in your hands. The game knows. You do not.

Objects that are only there to be carried

Here is the small oddity worth noticing: a great many adventure games include objects that do nothing at all. Not trap objects, not decoys with a joke attached, just things the game permits you to take and never asks about again. A pebble. An old boot. A book whose title is the whole of its content.

The reflex is to call this a design flaw, and sometimes it is. But the more interesting reading is that the useless object is doing quiet work. It teaches you that permission is not instruction. It puts a little noise into the signal, and the noise is what makes the real inventory feel like a decision rather than a receipt.

A game with only useful objects is a game that has told you everything in advance. A game with a few useless ones is admitting it has not.

The discipline of the empty hand

The player who understands this stops hoarding. This is not a tip, and it is not a habit worth teaching, exactly. It is something you learn by being wrong enough times: that the pile in the corner is not progress, that a full inventory can be a way of not thinking. The empty hand is the state in which you can actually look at a room.

Adventure games rarely say this. They reward accumulation loudly and letting go quietly. You can carry thirty objects and never be asked to drop one, and the game will be no worse for it, and you will be slightly worse, because you spent the second half of the story rummaging.

Why the form can say something the others cannot

Other kinds of games have inventories too, and they are mostly legible: the numbers tell you what an object is worth, and worth is a decision already made. A text adventure cannot lean on a number. It hands you a word and waits for you to decide what the word is for. The question is not how much it weighs but whether it belongs to the story you are in.

That is a strange thing to ask a player, and it is the reason the small useless objects matter so much. They force the question. The rope cannot tell you if it is the rope for this problem. Only you can, by the quality of your attention, and you will sometimes be wrong, and the world will not collapse. It will simply hold the rope until you are finished with it, or until you are not.

The ending you carry out

There is a moment near the end of most of these games when the inventory is nearly empty and the story is nearly done, and the two are not a coincidence. The obligations have been paid or abandoned. What is left in the list is what the game decided you could not leave behind, and the shape of that short list is the shape of the ending.

Which is why the useless objects are not a flaw and the hoarding is not really a strategy. They are the same lesson from two directions: a good adventure game is not about what you can pick up. It is about what you are willing to put down, and when, and whether you noticed the difference while there was still time to be wrong about it.
