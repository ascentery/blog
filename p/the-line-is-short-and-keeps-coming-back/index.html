The Line Is Short, and It Keeps Coming Back

28 November 2026

I want to talk about the queue. Not the crowd. The queue. Because every time someone announces that text adventures, MUDs or roguelikes are dead, what they really mean is that the queue is short. It is. That's the first honest thing I can tell you.

Here's the second, and it's less fun: I have no numbers. Nothing in this article was measured. Nobody handed me a chart of who logs in, how many, or on what evening. So I will not invent one, and you should be suspicious of anyone who does. We are going to reason from the shape of the thing instead.

The audience is a supply-side problem

A parser game asks you to type what you want. That is a strange bargain in a world of thumbnails. It also filters for exactly the kind of person who enjoys being filtered, which is a durable type. The audience is small because the door is narrow, not because the room is bad.

MUDs narrow it further: live humans, expected, on a schedule. A roguelike narrows it with permadeath. Each of these is a velvet rope made of patience. You don't stumble past a velvet rope. You decide to.

So the honest size of this audience is: whoever decides to be patient that week. It goes up and down. It does not go to zero, because patience never quite goes out of style.

The tools are quiet, and quiet isn't dead

Long-running free game-making tools exist. Communities still gather around them. People still build things in them and complain about them, and complaining about the tool is the highest known form of community loyalty. None of this is a growth story. It also isn't a funeral. It's a workshop with the lights on and somebody at the bench.

The case against, fairly

The cheery version says the audience got absorbed into other games, that every trick the form invented lives on elsewhere. Fine. Absorbed is a nice word for borrowed without asking. If your innovations are now standard in games with budget and marketing, the form that birthed them can feel like a museum of its own inventions. That's a real cost and I won't wave it away.

The harder problem is discovery. A narrow door is a problem when nobody can find the door. New players don't need a smaller audience; they need a sign.

What the evidence can't show

Nothing here was measured, so I can't tell you the queue is growing, shrinking or holding. I can tell you what the queue is made of, because the form tells me. Readers. Typers. People who like a locked door more than a hallway. People who will retype the same verb eleven times and call the eleventh time clever.

That's the true point hiding in all this. The audience for these games isn't large, and it was never going to be, and it keeps showing up because the door asks something of them and something asked of you is rarer than it used to be. The queue is short. Short is not empty.
