A Verb Is a Promise You Make to Yourself

27 November 2026

Every text adventure hands you the same instrument: a verb. Not a character, not a cutscene, not a hand on the shoulder guiding you toward the door. A verb, and a blank line underneath it, and the faint pressure of a room described in three sentences that expect something from you.

Here is the claim, and it is a strange one: the verb is not a tool. It is a promise. When you type take apple, you are not issuing a command to a game. You are telling yourself, quietly and in front of a witness, that you intend to be the kind of person who picks up the apple. The machine has no choice. The machine has already agreed. The only party who can break the promise is you.

The Apple Is Not the Point

Consider Shandara. The premise is almost rude in its simplicity: a thief, a city, a treasure room that is not going to open itself. The first honest move in that city is small. A wild apple sits half-buried in the trash of the Back Alley, and the entire climb from squalid streets to the treasure room begins with a verb aimed at a piece of fruit.

That is not a fetch quest. It is a rehearsal. The apple is a low-stakes promise, the kind you make to yourself on a Monday and keep. Take it, and you have declared something about how this run will go. Refuse it, and the game does not scold you. It waits, exactly as patient and exactly as indifferent as a locked door.

A locked door, as someone on this blog has already argued, is a question. Fair enough. But a verb is the answer you give before you know what was asked.

The Parser Is a Mirror With a Bad Memory

Now the mischief. In Miss Piggy Goes to the Market, the whole world runs on a sow gathering flour, butter, sugar, cinnamon, a rolling pin, and apples, with a sly Fox in a den under a fallen log and a grumpy Cow behind a dairy counter who sighs at you like you are the third disappointment of her morning.

The sow does not need to be brave. The sow needs to be specific. Ask Cow for butter works. Get butter from a cow who has not yet decided to like you is a verb aimed at a wall. And here the form does something no other medium quite does: it makes you revise your own sentence in real time, out loud, in the privacy of a text box.

You say a thing. The game says no. You say a slightly better thing. The game either says yes or says no in a way that teaches you something. This loop, repeated, is not a puzzle. It is a lesson in the difference between wanting and asking. The Fox calls everyone 'dearie' and the Old Goat speaks in riddles and the Hen clucks 'dear,' and none of that is decoration. It is the grammar of the promise. You learn who will accept which verb from whom.

Failure Is the Only Honest Teacher Here

A film cannot be failed. A locked door in a film opens when the plot needs it to. In a text adventure, the door is not a wall and it is not a plot device. It is a verdict on your sentence.

Theatrically speaking, this is the best trick in the form. The failure message is the only honest character in the room. It never flatters you. It never improvises a rescue. It simply reports that take apple from three rooms away does not reach, and lets the silence do the work.

That silence is where the player actually lives. Not in the treasure room. Not at the finish. In the half-second after you press return, before the screen decides whether to be generous. Everything worth knowing about a person's relationship to adventure games happens in that half-second.

The Host Knows This Too

There is a pixel-art host who sometimes plays these games on video, and watching someone else make a promise to a parser is its own small comedy. The host types look, and the room answers. The host types something reckless, and the room answers with a locked door and a faint suggestion that maybe the apple should have been taken first. The audience laughs, because they have all been that host. They have all typed a verb and then flinched.

Ascentery builds these worlds as you go, illustrated in the moment, which means the room is often described in the same breath as it is entered. The AI is not the point. The point is that the room was only ever waiting for a sentence, and the sentence was only ever yours.

What the Verb Actually Costs

So here is the sharp edge of it. A verb is cheap to type and expensive to mean. Every adventure game is a stack of small promises made by a stranger to a machine that has agreed in advance to take them seriously. The apple is not the apple. The verb is the vow. The locked door is the mirror. And the parser, bless it, has a terrible memory and endless patience, which is exactly the temperament of anyone who has ever decided to become a thief, or a sow, or a person who intends to keep the small promise after all.
